## 01 — My Story

*Not the path I was handed — the one I chose.*

I grew up in **Bhalwal, Sargodha**. Not as developed as Lahore, not as loud. It doesn't have everything — but it's mine and I love it. I loved computers before I knew what engineering was. When CE became an option, it was never a question.

First semester hit different from what I expected. All-nighters before mids and finals. PF and AICT labs built from scratch. A *full day navigating an unfamiliar Lahore to repair my laptop* — nothing to eat but a packet of grains the whole day, the bus back sold out but still running, then 4km on foot through Faisalabad, a proper meal when I finally reached my room. A Honahaar Scholarship trip with document complications I hadn't expected. A groupmate I got close to transferred to Lahore. I lost focus for a while. Then I studied harder than I ever had before.

I finished with a **CGPA of 3.91 — 2nd highest in my class**. Second semester is online now. Wars, petrol prices, Eid. Studying from home. But I'm still here, and I'm not stopping.

### 01 — The First Time I Loved Something That Kept Breaking

- How growing up loving computers meant nothing when Dr. Bilal's PF asked me to actually write Python for the very first time
- The build-break-debug feedback loop that made me fall in love with programming — frustration with a reason beats frustration with none
- Staying up past midnight rewriting code that already ran because running was not the same as being right

**Before UET, computers were easy**

I grew up in Bhalwal loving computers. Not in a studied, formal way — just in the way some people grow up loving football or cooking. I was always the person in the room who figured things out on screen faster than others. So when I got into BS Computer Engineering at UET Lahore Campus Faisalabad, I assumed that part of me would translate. It did not. Because there is a massive difference between using a computer and telling one what to do.

**What Programming Fundamentals actually was**

Dr. Bilal walked into the PF lecture calm but completely serious. From the first week, it was clear this subject was going to ask something that YouTube tutorials and casual computer use had never asked: precise, logical, structured thinking. Python looks simple until you are writing code that has to actually work — not approximately work, not kind of work — actually work. Every indentation matters. Every colon. Every variable name.

I loved it immediately. That surprised me. I expected frustration, and it was frustrating — but the frustration had a different quality. When Python broke, it broke for a reason I could track down. When it worked, it worked because I had done something right. That feedback loop — build, break, debug, understand, build again — became the thing I looked forward to most in the semester.

> *"When Python broke, it broke for a reason I could track down. That feedback loop became the thing I looked forward to most."*

**The nights that made it real**

The PF labs were where I spent most of my late nights in first semester. Not because I was struggling — but because I genuinely did not want to stop until the program worked the way I wanted it to. I cannot submit something I am not proud of. So I stayed up. I debugged things that worked but that I did not like the structure of. My roommate Muhammad Tahir Yaqoob would be asleep and I would still be at the desk, rearranging code that technically did not need rearranging. That habit — working until it felt right, not just until it ran — is the same thing that produced a 3.91 CGPA at the end of the semester. *And I would do it exactly the same way again.*

### 02 — The Calculus Quiz I Should Have Missed

- A smoggy winter morning, ironed clothes, forgotten glasses, and arriving 15 minutes late to a quiz that had already started
- How the ma'am gave me full marks I probably did not deserve — and what that grace taught me about assumptions and logic
- Sleeping through Fehm ul Quran lectures and getting a B: the honest relationship between showing up and what you earn

**The morning that went wrong in every possible way**

It was winter. Smog had been sitting over the area for days. I woke up late. The Calculus I ma'am had been arriving a bit late in recent weeks. I made the calculation, in my half-asleep brain, that she would probably be late again today. She was not late today. By the time I ironed my clothes — because I could not walk out in wrinkled clothes even in a crisis — and then discovered I had left my glasses in my room and had to go back for them, I was fifteen to twenty minutes late. The quiz had started.

> *"She was not late today. And I was still ironing my clothes."*

I got full marks. I am grateful. I am also aware that I got lucky in a way I should not rely on again. The real lesson: assumptions without evidence produce bugs. I slept through some Fehm ul Quran lectures — theoretical, early morning, brutal for a night owl. I got a B. I deserved a B. The semester taught me, through both cases, that being present is not optional.

### 03 — Applied Physics, 3 Marks in a Quiz, and What Came After

- Getting 3 marks on quiz one — not from not trying, but from approaching a theory-heavy subject with the wrong preparation method entirely
- How the Applied Physics lab gave me an A while the theory fought me all semester
- The adjustment before finals that pulled everything up to an A−

Applied Physics exposed a specific gap in how I learn. I am excellent at practical work and mathematics. But Applied Physics theory required rote understanding of concepts I found difficult to hold through memorization rather than application. Quiz one: three marks. Not from being unprepared — from being unprepared in a very specific and fixable way.

> *"Three marks. Not from not trying. From trying the wrong way."*

Before finals I stopped trying to understand Applied Physics the way I understood Python — through doing — and started treating it like a different problem requiring different preparation. The finals went well. A−. I am proud of that A− in a way I am not proud of easier grades. *That is the same skill that good debugging requires — find the actual root cause.*

### 04 — Mahnoor Left, and I Studied Like I Had Something to Prove

- How Mahnoor Fatima held the CR and GR role together — and what the batch lost when she transferred to Lahore mid-semester
- The drift that followed: joining the gym for a few days, going through motions, losing the sharpness
- How the studying I did in the second half became the best and most focused studying of my CE journey so far

Mahnoor Fatima had the highest aggregate in ECAT among us. She became CR and GR and she actually did the work. When Mahnoor transferred to Lahore, I lost focus for a few weeks — not completely, not visibly to anyone outside my own head, but I was going through motions rather than actually engaging.

> *"I lost focus for a few weeks. Not visibly. But I was going through motions rather than actually engaging."*

At some point something shifted. I started studying with a quality of focus I had not had earlier in the semester. The CGPA I ended with — 3.91, second in my class — was built largely in that second half, after Mahnoor left. *I do not think I would have worked that hard if nothing had first knocked me off balance.*

### 05 — 10 Hours for a Laptop, 4 Kilometres on Foot, and Still Home Before Midnight

- A full day navigating an unfamiliar Lahore for a laptop repair — nothing to eat all day except a single packet of grains
- The bus back was sold out but still ran — then 4km on foot through Faisalabad to reach the hostel
- A proper meal when I finally reached my room, then opening the laptop just to check

In a CE semester, your laptop is not a convenience. It is the workspace, the lab, the library, and the submission portal all in one. When mine started failing, I went to Lahore for repairs. The entire day, the only thing I had eaten was a single packet of grains. The bus back was sold out — but it still ran. I arrived in Faisalabad and walked around 4 kilometres to reach the hostel.

> *"A packet of grains for the whole day. Then 4km on foot. Then a proper meal. Then I opened the laptop — just to check everything was still there."*

It was. *Both Lahore trips — the laptop repair and the Honahaar Scholarship document submission — were a kind of education that no lab session could have provided.*

### 06 — The AICT Presentation, the Lab Reports, and the Printer That Was Never Available

- Choosing Seerat ul Nabi (PBUH) as my AICT presentation topic — and getting full marks on something that mattered beyond the grade
- The invisible labour of lab reports and the printer that was never available when needed
- Standing at the project exhibition defending the ML weather model to seniors

I chose Seerat ul Nabi (PBUH) for my AICT presentation. I made it carefully. The design was clean, the content organized, the delivery confident. I got full marks. The subject was personal — presenting on the life of the Prophet (PBUH) required care and respect that made me approach the preparation differently. *AICT was the course where I had to be both technical and human at the same time.*

The lab report required time separate from the lab work itself — writing, formatting, explaining the process. I took lab reports seriously, sometimes helping write my groupmates' reports too. Then the printer was unavailable. Sometimes broken. The cost of printing added up across a full semester. Logistics that had nothing to do with academic content.

> *"The lab report was one thing. Getting it onto paper and submitted on time was another thing entirely."*

At the project exhibition, seniors came to find the edge of what you understood and push past it. Standing there answering those questions — explaining the model, the dataset, the choices — was the most honest assessment of whether I actually understood what I had spent the semester building. I walked out knowing I did.

### 07 — Dr. Bilal Said No to Dropshipping and Accidentally Pointed Me Somewhere Better

- Proposing a dropshipping e-commerce site for the DBS project and getting clearly rejected
- Saying AI/ML without hesitating when offered the alternative
- How the rejection directly led to this portfolio project and the AI/ML direction I actually wanted

Dr. Bilal gave us the DBS project brief: build a working website grounded in local goods and local commerce. I came with a dropshipping platform sourcing from Daraz and Amazon. He said no — clearly and without much deliberation.

> *"He said no clearly. And then he gave me two options that changed the direction of my entire semester."*

He offered alternatives: write blog posts, or shift to AI/ML. I said AI/ML without hesitating. He asked: will you host your model on the cloud? I said yes. I walked out having committed to something I had never done before. Then classes moved online and Dr. Bilal shifted the class to this portfolio project and AI/ML work. *Dr. Bilal saying no to my first idea was, accidentally, pointing me precisely where I needed to go.*

### 08 — The Train Home, the Razai, and What Bhalwal Feels Like After Months Away

- Ramadan, online classes, regional wars, petrol prices — the moment second semester moved from hostel to home
- Taking the train back to Bhalwal for the first time with friends, carrying the razai
- Abdullah Khalid, Abdullah Sikandar, and the specific feeling of returning to a city that already knows you

Second semester started on campus and then, about four to five weeks in, shifted online — war between Iran, Israel, and America; tension between Afghanistan and Pakistan; petrol prices rising sharply. We went home. It was Ramadan. This trip home meant carrying the razai back. Blankets are big and awkward on a bus. They are not awkward on a train.

> *"The train was cheaper. The razai fit easily. My home is near the station. Some things, occasionally, just go right."*

I do not experience Bhalwal as smaller — I experience it as home. Abdullah Khalid and Abdullah Sikandar are there — my two closest friends. After months in the hostel, returning to Bhalwal feels like rest in a way that goes beyond being tired. It is the feeling of being somewhere that already knows you.

### 09 — What 3.91 Actually Looks Like From the Inside

- The real cost behind the number: a quiz with 3 marks, weeks of lost focus, a B in Fehm ul Quran, and 2 AM sessions nobody sees on a transcript
- The subjects where I found myself — PF labs I stayed late for voluntarily, AICT with Ma'am Munnazza, the Seerat ul Nabi presentation
- What the Honahaar Scholarship meant practically and personally

3.91 CGPA. Second highest in my class. The first was my groupmate, the third was also my groupmate. The number looks clean from the outside. A CGPA on a transcript does not show the quiz I got three marks in. It does not show the Fehm ul Quran B that came from sleeping in lectures while exhausted. It does not show the weeks I lost focus. It does not show the 2 AM sessions or the lab reports I rewrote because the first version was not something I was proud of.

> *"A CGPA does not show the quiz where I got 3 marks. Or the weeks I lost focus. Or the 2 AM sessions that rebuilt it."*

Programming Fundamentals was the subject I loved the most. Python clicked for me in the way that practical, logical work always clicks. The PF labs were where I spent the most voluntary time — where I stayed late not because I had to but because I wanted the program to be good.

The Honahaar Scholarship came from the grades. The trip to Lahore to submit the documents — the complications, the back-and-forth — was its own kind of test. I got through it. I needed it. The financial reality of being a student from Bhalwal living in a hostel is not negligible. *Gratitude and ambition are not contradictions. One is the acknowledgment of where I am. The other is the refusal to stop there.*

### 10 — What I Would Tell the Version of Me Who Arrived at UET Not Knowing the Routes

- On Programming Fundamentals: fall into it completely — stop when the code is right not when it runs
- On the hard things: the groupmate who leaves, the laptop trip on a packet of grains — all temporary except what they teach you
- On Database Systems in 2nd semester: when Dr. Bilal rejects your first idea, the replacement is always pointing somewhere better

Dear first-semester me. You are at the hostel. You do not know the bus routes yet. You have your laptop, your bag, and two best friends — Abdullah Khalid and Abdullah Sikandar — back in Bhalwal who you already miss more than you expected to. You are about to have a semester that is harder and better than anything you can currently imagine.

**On Programming Fundamentals:** You are going to love PF. Let yourself love it without reservation. When you stay up until 2 AM rewriting code that already works because you do not like its structure — that is not obsession, that is the right instinct.

> *"Stop when the program is right. Not when it runs. There is a difference, and it is everything."*

**On the hard things:** A groupmate you get close to is going to transfer to Lahore and you are going to lose focus for a while. The studying you do after she leaves is the best studying of the semester. The laptop repair trip — a full day, a single packet of grains, a sold-out bus that still runs, four kilometres on foot — that is going to happen too. You do not know yet that you are that kind of person. That trip will show you.

**On Database Systems:** Dr. Bilal is going to reject your dropshipping idea. Say AI/ML. Say it without hesitating. Commit to the cloud deployment before fully knowing what that means. You will find it. *Keep pulling it.*

You are going to be okay. More than okay.

*— Zain, 2nd semester, writing this from the same hostel, past midnight, train tickets already booked for next time.*
