The Database Systems project was supposed to be our chance to build something real. A full application. A proper database. Something that demonstrated everything we had learned about normalization, queries, and data modeling.

I had an idea ready: a dropshipping e-commerce platform.

It seemed like a solid plan. Dropshipping is a real business model. E-commerce requires a real database. I could build something that looked professional and functioned correctly. It checked every box.

Dr. Bilal said no.

Not "maybe," not "consider alternatives," not "let me think about it." A clear, direct no.

> "He said no clearly. And then he gave me two options that changed the direction of my entire semester."

His reasoning was simple: he wanted local production. He wanted projects that solved local problems, used local data, and demonstrated relevance to the context we actually live in. A dropshipping platform modeled on Daraz and Amazon was not that. It was a good technical exercise, but it was not the kind of project he was looking for.

I was disappointed at first. I had already started thinking about the architecture. I had already mentally committed to the idea. Getting rejected felt like losing momentum.

But then he gave me two options.

The first was an AI/ML-based project — something that used machine learning to solve a problem, not just store data. The second was a database project focused on a genuinely local use case, something that served a community or industry we actually interact with.

Both options were harder than dropshipping. Both required learning things I had not planned to learn. Both pushed me further than I had intended to go.

I chose the AI/ML direction.

And that choice changed everything.

Instead of building another e-commerce database, I started learning about machine learning models, training data, feature engineering, and prediction. Instead of working with a problem space I had no personal connection to, I was working on something that required actual research, actual understanding, actual innovation.

Dr. Bilal's rejection was not a dismissal. It was a redirection. He did not just say no to dropshipping — he said yes to something better, even if I did not see it at the time.

The AI/ML project was harder. It demanded more. It forced me to learn concepts that were outside my comfort zone and stay there until they became comfortable. It was the kind of challenge that makes you grow not because it is designed to be difficult, but because it is designed to be meaningful.

Looking back, I am grateful he said no. Not because dropshipping was a bad idea — it was a perfectly fine idea for a perfectly fine project. But I did not need a perfectly fine project. I needed one that would push me. One that would introduce me to AI/ML. One that would change the direction of my academic interests in a way that a dropshipping platform never could have.

Sometimes the best thing a mentor can do is reject your first idea. Not cruelly. Not dismissively. But clearly, and with something better in mind.

Dr. Bilal did exactly that. And the project that came after was better in every way that mattered.
