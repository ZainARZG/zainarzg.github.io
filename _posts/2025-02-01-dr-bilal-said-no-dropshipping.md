The Database Systems project was supposed to be our chance to build something real. A full application. A proper database. Something that demonstrated everything we had learned about normalization, queries, and data modeling.

I had an idea ready: a dropshipping e-commerce platform.

It seemed like a solid plan. Dropshipping is a real business model. E-commerce requires a real database. I could build something that looked professional and functioned correctly. It checked every box. I had already sketched out the entity-relationship diagram in my notebook — tables for products, users, orders, payments. I knew exactly how the foreign keys would connect.

Dr. Bilal said no.

Not "maybe," not "consider alternatives," not "let me think about it." A clear, direct no.

> "He said no clearly. And then he gave me two options that changed the direction of my entire semester."

His reasoning was simple: he wanted local production. He wanted projects that solved local problems, used local data, and demonstrated relevance to the context we actually live in. A dropshipping platform modeled on Daraz and Amazon was not that. It was a good technical exercise, but it was not the kind of project he was looking for.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** — a professor at UET Lahore, Faisalabad Campus, who specialises in AI, ML, and Deep Learning — has a consistent philosophy about student projects. He believes that the best learning happens when you work on problems that have real stakes. That is why he pushes students toward medical datasets whenever possible — because clinical data is collected with high precision and accuracy, and the problems it solves have measurable impact on real lives. A dropshipping platform, by contrast, solves a problem that has already been solved a hundred times over.

I was disappointed at first. I had already started thinking about the architecture. I had already mentally committed to the idea. Getting rejected felt like losing momentum. But then he gave me two options.

The first was an AI/ML-based project — something that used machine learning to solve a problem, not just store data. The second was a database project focused on a genuinely local use case, something that served a community or industry we actually interact with.

Both options were harder than dropshipping. Both required learning things I had not planned to learn. Both pushed me further than I had intended to go.

I chose the AI/ML direction.
The decision was not instant. I spent a few days going back and forth between the two options. The database project felt safer — I knew the tools, I understood the requirements, and I could execute it without learning an entirely new skillset. The AI/ML path was uncertain. I had never trained a machine learning model before. I did not know what feature engineering meant. The word "epoch" was not in my vocabulary at that point.

But I kept thinking about something **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** had said in one of his lectures. He was talking about how he trains ML models on medical datasets — echocardiograms, clinical records, diagnostic images — and how the models are only as good as the data they are trained on. He said: "If you want to build something that matters, start with data that matters." That sentence stuck with me. A dropshipping database, no matter how well built, would never make anyone's life better. An ML model that predicted something real might. His research on [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com.au/citations?user=8nZ0jVkAAAAJ&hl=en) — real-world ML applications — pushed me to choose the harder path.

So I chose the harder path.

That shift in thinking — from "what can I build comfortably" to "what should I build that matters" — was one of the most valuable takeaways from my entire semester with **Dr. Bilal Ahmad**. It applied not just to the DBS project but to every academic decision after it. I stopped asking whether a project was doable and started asking whether it was worthwhile.


And that choice changed everything.

Instead of building another e-commerce database, I started learning about machine learning models, training data, feature engineering, and prediction. My collaborator Muhammad Tahir Yaqoob — who was also my roommate — and I worked on a supervised learning approach that forecasted next-day temperature and precipitation using ten years of historical weather data from eighteen major European cities. We used the European Cities Weather Prediction Dataset from Kaggle, preprocessed the data, engineered features, trained the model, and evaluated its performance.


In many ways, that single "no" shaped my academic direction more than any "yes" could have. It forced me to confront the difference between a comfortable project and a meaningful one. **Dr. Bilal Ahmad's** emphasis on real-world relevance, local production, and high-quality data became the standard I now measure every project against.

Looking back, I am grateful he said no. Not because dropshipping was a bad idea — it was a perfectly fine idea for a perfectly fine project. But I did not need a perfectly fine project. I needed one that would push me. One that would introduce me to AI/ML. One that would change the direction of my academic interests in a way that a dropshipping platform never could have.

Sometimes the best thing a mentor can do is reject your first idea. Not cruelly. Not dismissively. But clearly, and with something better in mind.

Dr. Bilal did exactly that. And the project that came after was better in every way that mattered.
