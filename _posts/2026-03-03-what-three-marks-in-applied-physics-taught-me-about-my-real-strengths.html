The moment a number forced me to be honest

There are some results that are easy to forget because they fit the story you already want to tell yourself. Then there are results that interrupt that story completely. For me, one of those moments was getting only three marks in an Applied Physics quiz. The number itself was small, but the effect was large because it exposed something I had already started noticing in first semester: I am much stronger in practical, logical, and mathematical work than I am in subjects that depend heavily on memorization.

That quiz did not create the problem. It revealed it. By that point, I already felt more alive in Programming Fundamentals, lab work, and structured problem-solving than I did in theoretical material that asked for recall more than reasoning. But a low result makes the truth sharper. It removes excuses and forces you to decide whether you are going to panic, deny it, or learn from it.

Recovering without pretending the subject suddenly became easy

What mattered to me afterward is that I did not magically become someone who loved Applied Physics theory. I still found it harder than the subjects that matched my natural strengths. I did not suddenly enjoy memorization, and I did not dominate every remaining quiz or test. Some parts of the course stayed average. But I kept working, especially toward the finals, and I focused on recovering the overall result rather than turning one bad performance into the definition of the whole semester.

In the end, I finished Applied Physics theory with an A-. That mattered to me because it proved something important: difficulty and defeat are not the same thing. A subject can be uncomfortable, less natural, and still manageable if I approach it honestly. Recovery did not mean pretending it was my favorite course. It meant understanding the gap, adjusting the effort, and refusing to let one weak result decide the whole outcome.

Understanding my strengths more clearly

The bigger lesson from those three marks was not only about Applied Physics. It was about self-awareness. I learned that my best work usually happens where I can reason, test, and refine instead of only memorize. That is why programming, maths, and practical subjects feel more natural to me. But I also learned that knowing my strengths does not give me permission to ignore my weak areas. It gives me a more honest starting point for dealing with them.

I still think that quiz was useful, even though it was unpleasant. It showed me the difference between a subject that does not match me perfectly and a subject I cannot recover in. Those are not the same thing. Applied Physics made me work in a less natural mode, and that was frustrating, but it also taught me discipline in a different form. More importantly, it helped me define myself more accurately. I am strongest when the work is practical and structured, but I can still push through less natural material when I have to. That is a better lesson than one comfortable success.
