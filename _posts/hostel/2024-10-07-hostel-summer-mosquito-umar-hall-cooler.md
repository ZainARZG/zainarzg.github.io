The first summer in that room was when things got serious.

We had survived the dirt. We had survived the one wobbling fan. We had survived winter with a small heater and a chulha that barely cooked. We had made the room ours. But summer — summer was a different enemy.

It started with the mosquitos.

I do not know where they came from. Maybe the stagnant water near the hostel. Maybe the open drains behind the building. Maybe they had been there all along, waiting for the temperature to rise so they could emerge and claim the room. But one night, I woke up covered in bites. Arms. Legs. Neck. The kind of bites that itch for days, that swell into small red bumps, that make it impossible to sleep without scratching.

I bought a mosquito net the next day.

It was a simple net — white, rectangular, the kind you hang from a hook in the ceiling and tuck under your mattress at night. Installing it took longer than I expected. The ceiling was concrete, and the hooks kept slipping. But once it was up, it worked. The net became a barrier between us and the swarm. Sleeping under it felt like being in a tent, a small white bubble of safety in a room that was slowly becoming unbearable.

But the net could not fix the heat.

The top floor of the hostel was a heat trap. The sun hit the roof all day, and by evening, the room had absorbed every degree of it. The one fan pushed warm air in circles. The walls radiated heat. Sleeping was not restful — it was an endurance task, a fight between exhaustion and discomfort.

That is when I shifted to Umar Hall.

Umar Hall was a different building on the hostel campus — older, with thicker walls and bigger windows and a cross-breeze that the top floor never had. A friend of mine was already living there, and he told me I could stay for a while. I packed a bag, left the room, and moved into Umar Hall for two to three weeks.

It was cooler. Not by a little — by a lot. The difference was immediate. Walking into Umar Hall felt like walking out of an oven into shade. I could sleep through the night. I could study without sweat dripping onto my notebook. I could exist in a room without counting the minutes until I could escape it.

I lived there with my friend, sharing his space, sharing his fan, sharing the quiet relief of a room that did not fight against you. Two to three weeks of borrowed comfort.

But I could not stay forever.

Umar Hall was not my room. I had to go back eventually — to my own bed, my own shelf, my own mosquito net. The heat was waiting for me. So I did the only thing that made sense: I bought an air cooler.

A desert cooler, they call it sometimes. A box with cooling pads and a fan and a pump that circulates water. You fill it with water, switch it on, and it pushes cool air into the room. It is not air conditioning — it does not make the room cold. But it makes the room survivable. It drops the temperature by a few degrees, just enough to sleep, just enough to breathe.

Without that cooler, we would not have made it through the summer in that room.

It sat in the corner, humming all night, its water tank needing a refill every few hours. We learned to keep a bucket of water next to it. We learned to time our refills so we would not run out at 3 AM. We learned that survival is not about grand solutions — it is about small, repetitive acts of maintenance.

And then the second semester ended.

It had been a long semester — OOP and DBS and the e-commerce project and Dr. Bilal's ML lectures and the quizzes and the finals. We had survived all of it. But the room was still on the top floor, and I knew I could not face another summer there.

So I went to the resident tutor.

I had never spoken to him before. He was a quiet man, rarely seen in the corridors, the kind of authority figure you only notice when you need something. I knocked on his door, explained the situation — the top floor, the heat, the cooler that barely kept up. I asked if there was any way to move.

He listened. Nodded. Said he would see what he could do.

A few days later, he came back with the answer. We could move. There was a room available on the ground floor — cooler, better air circulation, away from the direct heat of the roof. It was not a bigger room. It was not a newer room. But it was lower, and lower meant cooler, and cooler was all I wanted.

Thank God. I said it then and I mean it now.

The new room — the one we will move into after the break — is on the ground floor. I have seen it only once, briefly, just to confirm it existed. It felt different from the top floor. The air moved differently. The light was softer. It felt like a room that would not fight us.

Now it is summer break. I am home, writing this, thinking about the journey of that first room. The dirt. The one fan. The mosquito net. The borrowed weeks in Umar Hall. The air cooler. The resident tutor who listened. The new room waiting for us.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** talks about hyperparameter tuning — the process of adjusting the settings of a model until it performs well in its environment. One learning rate. One batch size. One architecture decision at a time, until the model converges. That is what we did with the room. Mosquito net. Umar Hall. Air cooler. Room change request. One adjustment at a time, until the living situation converged to something that worked.

We will return after the break and start again in the new room. Ground floor. Cooler air. A fresh start.
