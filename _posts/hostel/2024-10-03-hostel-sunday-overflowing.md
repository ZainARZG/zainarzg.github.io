Two days after the bee bite, we came back.

This time it was Sunday — the official moving day, the day when hostels open, when students arrive from every corner of the region, when the campus finally wakes up after months of silence. We thought we knew what to expect. We had seen the campus empty, walked its silent corridors, stood at its locked gates. We had already had our first disappointment. This time, we were prepared.

We were not prepared.

The scene at the hostel gate was nothing like the empty parking lot we had stood in two days earlier. There were students everywhere. Cars, rickshaws, vans pulling up with bags strapped to their roofs. Families saying goodbye. Students in groups, carrying mattresses, pillows, suitcases, looking around with the same mix of excitement and uncertainty that we were feeling. The queue at the registration desk stretched out the door. The noise was constant — voices, engines, the sound of footsteps on concrete.

I remember stopping for a moment just to take it in. Two days ago, this place had felt abandoned. Now it felt like a city had arrived overnight.

We pushed through the crowd toward the first hostel. The line was long, but we waited, thinking that as long as we were early enough, we would get a room. When we finally reached the desk, the answer was brief: full. Every bed taken. The first hostel had reached capacity before we even got through the door.

We did not stop to process it. We turned around and moved to the second hostel.

The second hostel was further from the main gate, tucked behind the academic buildings, quieter than the first. We walked faster this time, cutting through groups of students, hoping that the second hostel would have space where the first one did not. When we reached the registration desk, the answer was the same: full.

But then the person at the desk paused, looked at a piece of paper, and said something I still remember clearly: "You are the last ones."

The last ones. The final two beds in the second hostel. The very end of the capacity. If we had arrived five minutes later — if we had stopped to buy water, if we had taken a wrong turn, if we had waited in the first queue a little longer — we would have been standing outside with nowhere to go.

We took the beds without asking any questions. There was no time to check the room, no time to compare options, no time to think about whether this was the best choice. The choice was simple: take what was available or sleep outside.

The room was small, cramped, and already filled with the bags of our new roommates. But it had a bed. That was enough.

What happened after we got our beds is something I think about whenever I hear someone romanticise university life. The students who came after us — the ones who arrived five minutes late, the ones who got stuck in traffic, the ones whose buses arrived after ours — were given a different arrangement. The university had run out of rooms. So they opened a hall. A large, open hall with no partitions, no privacy, and no individual beds. They put ten to fifteen students in that single hall, each with a mattress on the floor, their bags piled in the corners, their lives compressed into the space between one sheet and the next.

I walked past that hall later that evening. The lights were fluorescent, harsh, the kind that makes everyone look tired even when they are not. The students inside were unpacking their things, trying to mark their territory on the floor, arranging their mattress at an angle that gave them the illusion of personal space. Some of them were talking, laughing, trying to make the best of it. Others were sitting quietly, staring at their phones, processing the reality of what their first night in university would look like.

I did not say anything to them. What could I have said? I was one of the lucky ones — I had a bed in a room, not a mattress on a hall floor. The line between my situation and theirs was a matter of minutes.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** talks about this in his ML lectures in a way that has stuck with me: the difference between a model that generalises well and one that overfits is often just the quality of the data it was trained on. The same is true for students. The ones who got the hall were not worse students. They were not less prepared. They simply had noisier data to start with — a louder environment, less space, more distractions. And yet, some of them would go on to outperform those of us who got beds. Because the quality of the training environment matters, but it is not the only variable.

I lay in my bed that night, in a room that was too small, with roommates I had not met until that afternoon, and thought about the hall down the corridor. I thought about the students on mattresses on the floor, and I thought about what **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** says about noisy data producing robust models. If that was true — and I believed it was — then the students in that hall were going to be the most robust of all of us.
