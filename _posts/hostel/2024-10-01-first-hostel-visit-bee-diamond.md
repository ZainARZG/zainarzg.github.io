The day we arrived at the hostel, nothing was ready.

We had travelled from Bhalwal with a single goal: reach the university early, secure a room, and make sure our admission went through without any last-minute surprises. That was the plan. The kind of plan that sounds reasonable when you are sitting at home, imagining how university life will begin. The kind of plan that falls apart the moment you step out of the bus and realise the hostel is locked.

Not just locked. Empty. Unopened. The kind of silence that tells you the building has been waiting longer than you have.

We stood outside the gate, bags in hand, looking at a campus that was not ready to receive us. The hostels were not open yet. There was no one at the reception desk, no senior students to guide us, no signs pointing toward where we needed to go. Just a locked gate, a dusty driveway, and the sound of insects in the grass.

I had imagined this moment differently. In my head, arriving at the hostel was supposed to feel like the start of something — a new chapter, a fresh beginning, the kind of scene they show in university orientation videos where students walk through the gates smiling, bags slung over their shoulders, ready to take on the world. What I got instead was a locked gate and a growing sense that I had misunderstood something fundamental about how this worked.

We had assumed that arriving early would give us an advantage. First come, first served — that is how hostels work, right? Show up before everyone else, pick the best room, settle in before the rush. We had planned for that. What we had not planned for was the possibility that the hostel simply was not accepting anyone yet. There was a schedule, a system, a process — and our early arrival did not fit into it.

So we did what anyone would do when their plan collapses in the first hour: we started walking.

The campus was empty. Not the peaceful kind of empty, but the kind that makes every footstep echo. We wandered through the corridors, peered into locked classrooms, stood in the middle of the courtyard and tried to imagine what it would look like when it was full of students. The buildings were functional — concrete, practical, no unnecessary design. The hostel blocks stood at the edge of the campus, surrounded by open land that stretched further than I could see.

It was remote. More remote than I had expected.

The university was not in the middle of the city. It was on the outskirts, surrounded by fields and empty roads and the kind of quiet that only exists when civilisation is a twenty-minute drive away. The nearest market was a walk. The nearest city centre was a bus ride. Everything about the location told me that this was not going to be the university experience I had seen in movies or imagined in my head.

And then a bee bit me.

Through my pants.

I was standing near the hostel gate, looking at the empty building, trying to figure out what to do next — and I felt a sharp sting on my leg. I swatted instinctively, but the damage was done. A bee had found me, targeted me, and bitten me through the fabric of my pants. Not on exposed skin. Through fabric. As if the universe wanted to make sure I understood that this place was not going to make anything easy.

I remember looking at my friends and saying, completely seriously: "If we can live here, we can live anywhere."

It was not a joke. It was a realisation. If the hostel was locked, the campus was remote, and the local wildlife was already attacking us through our clothing — then anything that came after this could only be easier. We had arrived with expectations, and the first hour had demolished them. But that was fine. Because if this was the baseline, then every day after this would be a step up.

I said something else too, standing there with a bee sting on my leg and an empty campus around me: "We will become diamonds after grinding here."

It sounded dramatic at the moment. Maybe it was. But there was truth in it. A diamond is not made in comfortable conditions. It forms under pressure, deep underground, over time. This place — this remote, locked, bee-infested campus — was going to be the pressure. And if we survived it, we would come out the other side as something we could not have become anywhere else.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** talks about this in his ML lectures: the best models are not the ones trained on clean, perfect datasets. They are the ones trained on noisy, real-world data that forces them to generalise. The same is true for people. The rough start, the locked gate, the bee sting — all of it was noisy data. But noisy data produces robust models. And we were going to be robust.

We did not get our rooms that day. We went home, waited for the hostel to officially open, and returned later when the gates were finally unlocked. But I have never forgotten that first visit — the locked gate, the empty corridors, the bee that bit me through my pants, and the certainty I felt that this place would either break us or make us into something we could not have become anywhere else.

It made us.
