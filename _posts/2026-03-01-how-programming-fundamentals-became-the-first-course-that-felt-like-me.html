The first subject that matched how I think

Programming Fundamentals was the first course in university that felt less like a requirement and more like a fit. I had liked computers and technology since childhood, but before PF that interest was mostly emotional. I enjoyed the idea of computing, but I had not yet experienced what it feels like to sit with a problem, write logic for it, break that logic, and slowly make it better. When that started happening in Python, something clicked for me very early.

What made PF different was that it rewarded clarity. If I misunderstood something, the mistake showed up directly. If I improved the logic, the result improved with it. There was a fairness in that process that I connected with immediately. In some subjects I often felt that effort and outcome were separated by memorization, timing, or luck. In PF, the connection felt visible. I could see why something failed and I could usually trace a path toward fixing it.

Confidence built through repetition, not instant success

I do not think PF felt natural because it was easy. It felt natural because it made sense to stay with the problem. I remember spending long hours trying to get small things right and feeling strangely satisfied by it instead of drained. That mattered. It showed me that the kind of work I enjoy is often the kind that gets better through revision. I was not only learning syntax. I was learning patience, structure, and the difference between code that runs and code that actually makes sense.

The subject also showed me something about my own habits. I am a perfectionist in the sense that I do not like turning in work that feels careless. PF brought that side of me out strongly. I wanted my programs to feel clean, readable, and properly thought through. Sometimes that meant spending more time than necessary, but it also meant I began taking ownership of my work instead of treating assignments like boxes to tick.

Why it still matters to me

Looking back, PF gave me more than a good result in first semester. It gave me early proof that I belong in Computer Engineering. It helped me understand why practical subjects suit me more than purely theoretical ones. I perform better when the work demands reasoning, testing, and correction instead of only memorization. That realization has shaped how I now think about later subjects like Database Systems, where structure matters in a different but related way.

Even now, when I think about what pushed me from general interest into real commitment, PF is the course I return to first. It was the subject that made the field feel real. It gave me confidence at a time when I was still adjusting to hostel life, pressure, and the rhythm of university. More importantly, it introduced me to a kind of work that I could imagine doing seriously for a long time. That is why it still feels personal to me. It was the first course that did not just test me. It recognized something in me.
