When an idea stopped being random

My first serious project in university did not begin in a polished or structured way. It started with something much smaller: a conversation about weather that stayed in my head longer than I expected. At some point I had been talking to ChatGPT about something connected to weather, data, or code, and the topic did not seem important at the time. Later, when my teacher talked about training a model, that earlier thought suddenly connected with the coursework in a more serious way. That was the moment when the idea stopped being random and started feeling usable.

The project eventually became a weather prediction model focused on next-day temperature and precipitation forecasting in Basel using long-range weather data from European cities. What mattered to me was not only the project title or the technical result. What mattered was the feeling that an idea I had noticed casually could be turned into something structured, testable, and real. That is one of the reasons the project stayed important to me.

What made it feel serious

I worked on the project with Muhammad Tahir Yaqoob, and the collaboration was smooth partly because he was also my roommate. That made communication easier than it otherwise might have been. But even with that advantage, the project still felt different from routine lab work. It required me to think beyond simple completion. I had to see the whole shape of the work: the dataset, the training process, the evaluation, the logic behind the result, and the way we would explain it to others.

The technical side mattered, of course. The project used Python and a real machine learning workflow rather than a purely theoretical discussion. But what made it feel serious to me was the responsibility attached to it. It was not enough to say we had built something. We had to understand what it did, how it worked, and why it deserved to be presented as a real result. That changed my attitude toward project work in general.

Why the project changed how I saw myself

This project also opened a door in the way I think about my future. Before it, AI and machine learning were interesting to me in a broad, distant way. After it, those areas felt closer to my own path. I could imagine myself not only reading about such work but participating in it. That shift matters. It is one thing to admire a field. It is another thing to feel that you have taken your first real step inside it.

When I look back at first semester, this is one of the moments that stands out because it joined curiosity with action. The idea began informally, but it ended as something that required technical discipline, teamwork, presentation, and confidence. That combination is why I still think of it as my first serious project. It gave me a more practical sense of what it means to build something worth explaining, defending, and remembering after the semester ends.
