The semester-end project exhibition was set up in the main hall. Each course had its own row of tables, and students stood behind their projects, ready to explain, demonstrate, and defend. Three courses meant three projects. Three tables. And, as it turned out, a lot of standing.

The first table was for Digital Logic Design. Our ambulance priority traffic system was laid out on a breadboard — the 74LS148 priority encoder, the 74LS74 D Flip-Flop, the 555 Timer, the logic gates, the LEDs, the buzzer. It was the most physically visible project of the three, wires and ICs spread across the table, and it drew attention immediately. Teachers from other departments stopped by. Seniors from upper semesters picked up the breadboard, examined the wiring, and asked how the priority encoding worked. One senior asked why we used a hardware-only approach instead of a microcontroller. I explained that the constraint was intentional — the course was Digital Logic Design, not embedded programming, and the point was to prove that classical logic could solve real infrastructure problems without a single line of code. He nodded and said it made sense.

The DBS table was next, and this one was different. My groupmate was not there — something had come up, and I was alone. The laptop sat open with the FastAPI dashboard running, the MySQL schema diagram pinned to the display board beside it, and the Ridge Regression results printed on a sheet. I was nervous about being the only one to explain a project that had taken two people months to build. But before I could worry too much, the Head of Department walked over.

He stopped at our table, looked at the display board, then at me, and asked a single question: "Explain this project."

There was no one else to share the question with. No groupmate to jump in. Just me, the HOD, and a table full of work that I had done half of. I started from the beginning — the rejected TTS idea, the cBioPortal dataset, the 11 normalized tables, the Ridge Regression model, the FastAPI dashboard. He listened without interrupting. When I finished, he asked one more question about the normalization process, I answered, and he walked away. No praise. No criticism. Just attention. But the fact that he had stopped at our table at all, and that he had listened to the entire explanation, felt like acknowledgment enough.

The third table was for the OOP project — the Unity game. This was the one I was most excited to show. The laptop was running the RPG framework I had built, with the health system, the ScriptableObject inventory, and the event-driven communication between components. I was mid-demonstration with a group of classmates when the HOD appeared again.

He watched the demo for a moment, then asked whose work it was. I said I had built it. He looked at the screen, at the game running, and said something I had not expected: "This is good work." Then he left.

Two words. But from the Head of Department, at a project exhibition where dozens of projects were competing for attention, it meant everything. He had not stopped at every table. He had not praised every project. He had walked past most of them. But he had stopped at ours — at the DBS table where I stood alone, and at the OOP table where the game was running — and in both cases, he had engaged.

After he left the Unity table, I stood there for a moment, processing what had just happened. The semester had been exhausting — three major projects, sleepless nights, debugging sessions that stretched past midnight, and a groupmate who had transferred out mid-way through one of them. Standing at three different tables in one afternoon, explaining three completely different systems to whoever stopped by, felt like a summary of everything I had learned.

The DLD project had taught me to design before building. The DBS project had taught me that data credibility matters more than technical flashiness. The OOP project had taught me that clean architecture makes complex systems manageable. And the exhibition itself taught me something else: that explaining your work to someone who has never seen it is a skill as important as building it in the first place.
