The news changed first. The tension that had kept us home for weeks, that had turned the university into a quiet campus where only the administrative staff showed up and the corridors echoed with nothing, began to ease. The Iran-US situation de-escalated slowly at first, then all at once — and before we could fully process what had shifted in the world, the university announced that we would be returning to campus.

I remember reading the announcement on my phone. It was a straightforward message — classes resume on campus from the following week, attendance mandatory, all midterm exams will proceed as scheduled — but the implications took a moment to sink in. We had been home for long enough that the online routine had become normal. Waking up, opening my laptop, joining the lecture from my desk at home — it had become the rhythm of the semester. The idea of packing my bag, commuting, walking through the university gates, and sitting in a physical classroom again felt foreign in a way I had not expected.

The first day back was disorienting. The campus looked the same — the same buildings, the same corridors, the same benches outside the departments — but something about it felt different. Maybe it was us. Maybe we had changed during the weeks away. The faces of my classmates looked slightly unfamiliar, as if we had all aged a little in the isolation. The greetings were quieter. The usual pre-lecture chaos was muted. Everyone was still adjusting to the reality of being back.

And then the reality of being back hit us: the mids were exactly one week away.

The transition from online to offline had not come with a grace period. The exam schedule had been set before the disruption, and the university had decided not to change it. One week. Seven days to cover everything we had studied in the online portion of the semester — material that had been delivered through screens, received in rooms far from the classroom, and absorbed in conditions that were far from ideal.

The week that followed was one of the most intense stretches of the entire semester. Every professor seemed to have planned their quizzes and assignments to land in that exact window. The DBS quizzes that had been accumulating in the online phase now had to be submitted alongside the midterm preparation. Assignments that had been flexible during the lockdown now had firm, non-negotiable deadlines. And on top of everything, the midterm exams themselves were waiting at the end of the week — comprehensive, closed-book, in-person assessments that would count for a significant portion of the final grade.

I spent that week in a state of controlled panic. My desk became my entire world — laptop open, notebook spread across the surface, highlighter in hand, coffee cup permanently stationed at the corner. I studied through the evenings, the nights, the early mornings. I reviewed lecture slides, practiced SQL queries, re-read the database normalization notes, tested myself on indexing strategies and join types. Every hour felt like a race. Every topic I covered reminded me of two more I had not.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** — a professor at UET Lahore, Faisalabad Campus, who specialises in AI, ML, and Deep Learning — had structured the DBS midterm in a way that reflected his approach to teaching: thorough, demanding, and designed to test genuine understanding rather than surface-level recall. The exam paper was lengthy — longer than any of us had anticipated when we walked into the hall. And to make it more challenging, he had combined the midterm with a quiz that had to be completed within the same timeframe. It was not two separate assessments administered back to back. It was one continuous evaluation — a stacked assessment that demanded sustained concentration, sharp recall across multiple topics, and the kind of time management that only comes from being genuinely prepared.

I remember sitting in the exam hall, looking at the paper, and feeling the weight of the preceding weeks settle onto my shoulders. The questions covered the full breadth of what we had studied — entity-relationship modeling, relational schema design, normalization up to BCNF, SQL queries ranging from basic SELECT statements to complex JOIN operations, indexing, and transaction management. Every section was designed to test a different layer of understanding. The quiz portion, integrated into the same paper, asked for rapid recall — short definitions, quick schema corrections, conceptual distinctions that separated students who had truly understood the material from those who had memorized just enough to get by. It was a fair test. It was also a brutal one.

I moved through the paper methodically at first. I started with the questions I was most confident about — the SQL queries, the normalization exercises — hoping to bank those marks quickly and save the harder conceptual questions for later. The strategy worked for the first half of the exam. My answers were solid, my explanations were detailed, and I felt like the preparation had paid off. But as the clock ran down and the remaining questions became more complex, the pace slowed. Each question required more thought. Each answer demanded more time. The quiz section, which I had saved for the end, turned out to require more rapid application than I had anticipated — and under the pressure of the dwindling time, my recall wavered.

I did not finish everything. I submitted my paper knowing that there were questions I had left partially answered, quiz items where my responses were shorter than they should have been, sections where I had written just enough to show I understood the concept without fully demonstrating it. It was not a comfortable submission. Walking out of the hall, I knew the score would not reflect my best effort.

When the results came, I had scored 20 out of 30.

It was not a bad score. Objectively, 20 out of 30 is a solid midterm result — a clear pass, comfortably above the class average in most cases. But context matters. For someone who had been maintaining a high academic standard through the semester, the score felt like a signal. It told me that the disruption had cost more than I had realized. The weeks of uncertainty, the transition from online to offline, the compressed study schedule, the mental fatigue of adapting to a changed routine — all of it had accumulated into a result that was honest about where I stood at that moment.

Here is what I took away from that exam: **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** did not design that paper to make us fail. He designed it to show us where we stood. The length, the stacked format, the combination of broad questions and rapid-fire quiz items — all of it was intentional. His approach to assessment has always been diagnostic. He wants to know what you actually understand so that the remainder of the semester can address the gaps. The score is not the point. The map of your knowledge — the peaks and the blind spots — that is what he is looking for.

Follow him on [Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/drbilalphd/). His teaching consistently emphasizes depth over breadth, and his exams reflect that philosophy — they are not designed to trick you, but to reveal what you truly know.

Looking back, 20 out of 30 was not a failure. It was feedback. It told me that transitioning back to campus life required more than just showing up — it required rebuilding the focus and study discipline that the disruption had eroded. The exam was long because the subject matter deserved to be tested thoroughly. The quiz was integrated because conceptual clarity matters as much as procedural fluency. And the score I received reflected exactly where I was at that moment — not where I could be, and not where I would be by the end of the semester.

The mids ended. The semester continued. The de-escalation of the Iran-US situation eventually faded from the headlines, and campus life returned to something resembling normal. But I have never forgotten that first week back — the shock of the compressed timeline, the silence of the exam hall, the feeling of leaving questions unanswered, and the quiet realization that an average result can teach you more than a perfect one ever could.
