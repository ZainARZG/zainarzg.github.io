The holidays had started, but they did not feel like holidays. The news was full of the Iran-US escalation — whatever was happening between the two countries had made everything feel unstable, and like many other families, mine had decided that being home was better than being anywhere else. The university had already moved to online mode for safety. We were scattered across our cities, attending classes from our rooms, and the DBS lab evaluation arrived in the middle of all of it.

It was Ramadan.

Anyone who has experienced a Ramadan as a student knows that the month rewrites your entire schedule. You wake up before sunrise for sehri. You spend the daylight hours fasting, which means your energy dips in the afternoon. You break your fast at iftar. And then, at night, when the world is quiet and your body is running on the last meal of the day, you try to study — knowing that your brain is operating at maybe seventy percent of its normal capacity.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** had scheduled the DBS lab evaluation for 7 PM. In normal circumstances, 7 PM would have been a reasonable time — evening, post-class, everyone settled. But in Ramadan, 7 PM was a strange hour. It was after iftar, which meant we had just eaten, and the post-iftar energy dip was real. It was also the time when most of us would normally be resting or preparing for the night ahead. Instead, we were logging into an online evaluation, hoping our SQL queries would compile and our database designs would make sense.

I had planned to prepare the night before. I had my notes spread out on the desk — the ER diagrams, the normalization forms, the join types, the query patterns. I had opened my laptop at around 11 PM, determined to go through everything one more time so that the evaluation would feel like routine rather than pressure. **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** — a professor at UET Lahore, Faisalabad Campus, who specialises in AI, ML, and Deep Learning — had a reputation for thorough evaluations. He did not ask surface-level questions. He asked about the logic behind your design choices. He wanted to know why you chose one relationship over another, why you normalized to third normal form instead of stopping at second, why your query returned specific columns and not others. I knew I needed to be prepared beyond just remembering syntax.

I did not make it past midnight.

The combination of Ramadan, the holiday schedule, and the general mental exhaustion of the past few weeks caught up with me. I was reading through my notes on different types of joins — INNER, LEFT, RIGHT, FULL OUTER — and somewhere between understanding how a self-referencing foreign key worked and trying to remember the exact syntax for creating an index, I fell asleep. Not gradually. Not in a way where I could fight it. One moment I was reviewing, and the next moment I was waking up to the sound of my alarm telling me it was time for sehri.

I sat up groggily, disoriented, and realized that I had not finished preparing. The notes were still open on my laptop, the screen dimmed from inactivity. I ate sehri quickly — whatever was available, because there was no time to think about food when my brain was already running through SQL queries — and as soon as I finished, I sat back down at my desk and opened my laptop again.

The hours between sehri and the evaluation were a blur. I was reviewing while my brain was still half-asleep, trying to compress an entire week of lab work into a few focused hours of revision. The concepts were familiar — I had done the assignments, written the queries, designed the schemas — but the confidence was not there. I kept second-guessing myself. Was my understanding of third normal form correct? Had I handled the many-to-many relationship properly in the practice schema? Would the evaluation ask about indexing, or was that too advanced for this stage? I re-read my notes on functional dependencies, candidate keys, and the difference between BCNF and 3NF — things I knew but could not afford to slip on under pressure.

At 7 PM, I logged into the evaluation.

It did not go well.

And I was not alone in that. Everyone in the batch struggled. The timing, the Ramadan fatigue, the disrupted study schedules — all of it showed in the way people answered. The queries were slow to come. The explanations were hesitant. I remember staring at one particular question about join types, knowing the difference between an INNER JOIN and a LEFT JOIN at a conceptual level, but second-guessing myself under the pressure and giving an answer that was technically correct but incomplete. It was not my best work. It was not anyone's best work. The chat was quiet in a way that told me everyone was facing the same struggle.

But **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** did something that I still remember clearly: he ignored it.

Not in a dismissive way. Not in a way that made us feel like our effort did not matter. He simply did not penalize us for what had clearly been a difficult evaluation under difficult circumstances. He moved on. He treated the evaluation as a learning exercise rather than a high-stakes assessment. He understood that the combination of Ramadan, the geopolitical situation, the online transition, and the disrupted schedules had created conditions where nobody could perform at their best — and he adjusted accordingly.

That moment taught me something that has stayed with me longer than any SQL syntax: evaluation is not just about measuring what students know. It is also about understanding the context in which they are learning. A model trained on biased data produces biased results — **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** says this often in his ML lectures. A student evaluated under unfair conditions produces unfair results. The same principle applies. He recognized that and chose grace over rigidity.

Connect with him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/drbilalphd/). That flexibility — knowing when to hold the line and when to let things go — is one of the most valuable lessons I learned from that semester.

Looking back, I am grateful for that evaluation. Not because I performed well — I did not — but because it showed me that mentorship is not about enforcing rules uniformly. It is about reading the room, understanding the circumstances, and making decisions that prioritize learning over measurement. Sometimes the best thing a teacher can do is ignore a bad evaluation and let the student learn from the experience rather than be punished by it.

Ramadan ended. The semester continued. The Iran-US situation eventually de-escalated. But I have never forgotten the evaluation that started at 7 PM in the middle of a fasting month, and the teacher who chose to look past it.
