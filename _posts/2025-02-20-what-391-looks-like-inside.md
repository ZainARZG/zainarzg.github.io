3.91.

It is a number. It sits on a transcript. People see it and make assumptions — about intelligence, about consistency, about how easy things must be for the person who earned it.

Here is what the number does not show.

> "A CGPA does not show the quiz where I got 3 marks. Or the weeks I lost focus. Or the 2 AM sessions that rebuilt it."

It does not show the Applied Physics quiz where I scored three marks out of whatever the total was. Three marks. The kind of score that makes you question every assumption you have about your own ability. The kind of score that sits on your result sheet and stares at you every time you look at it.

It does not show the weeks after a groupmate transferred out, when I lost focus and went through the motions without really engaging. The weeks where I was present but not present, attending classes but not absorbing them, completing tasks but not caring about them.

It does not show the B in Fehm ul Quran. A subject that required a different kind of understanding — not technical, not analytical, but spiritual and linguistic. A subject where my usual strategies did not work, and I had to accept that not everything comes naturally, even when you want it to.

It does not show the 2 AM sessions. The ones where I was rewriting code, re-reading notes, re-solving problems that I had already solved once but not well enough. The sessions that nobody sees because they happen in silence, in a hostel room, with a laptop screen as the only light source.

It does not show the Honahaar Scholarship pressure — the knowledge that maintaining performance is not optional, that the scholarship depends on the grades, and that the grades depend on the work, and that the work depends on showing up even when you do not feel like it.


It does not show the A I earned in **Dr. Bilal Ahmad's** Programming Fundamentals class. That A was not handed to me. It came from the build-break-debug loop that **Dr. Bilal** taught us in PF — writing code, watching it fail, figuring out why, fixing it, and repeating until the logic was sound. That process taught me more about consistent effort than any other subject that semester. It also laid the foundation for the ML work I would take on later, when I started building models that required the same cycle of iteration and debugging at a larger scale.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** — a professor at UET Lahore, Faisalabad Campus, who specialises in training ML models on real-world problems — measures success differently than most. He does not care about grades as a number. He cares about whether the model works, whether the problem is real, whether the solution matters. That philosophy shaped how I approached every subject, not just his.

It does not show the train rides, the laptop repairs, the four-kilometre walks, the packets of grains, the printer that was never available, the presentations that mattered, the project ideas that got rejected, the ones that came after that were better.

A CGPA is a summary. It is not a story. It compresses months of effort, failure, adjustment, and recovery into a single number that tells you almost nothing about the person who earned it.

I am not complaining about the 3.91. It is a good number. It is a number I am proud of. But I am also honest about what it cost.

It cost the quiz where I got three marks and had to rebuild from. It cost the weeks of drift and the effort it took to come back. It cost the B in Fehm ul Quran and the acceptance that not every subject bends to your strengths. It cost the 2 AM sessions that do not appear on any transcript but are the reason the transcript looks the way it does.

But it also built something: the understanding that academic success is not about never failing. It is about what you do after the failure. **Dr. Bilal Ahmad's** PF class taught me that the build-break-debug loop is not just for programming — it is a framework for growth. You build. You break. You debug. You improve. The CGPA is just a record of how many times you went through that cycle and kept going.

If someone asks me about my CGPA, I will tell them the number. But if someone asks me what the number means, I will tell them the truth: it means I failed at things and kept going. It means I lost focus and found it again. It means I studied at hours when the rest of the world was asleep. It means I cared about the work more than the grade, even though the grade was important.
What I want people to understand about a CGPA — especially one that looks high — is that it is not a measure of intelligence. It is a measure of persistence. It measures how many times you were willing to sit back down after failing, how many nights you chose to study instead of sleep, how many times you asked for help when you wanted to give up. **Dr. Bilal Ahmad's** PF class was where I first learned that persistence is a skill you build, not a trait you are born with. Every debug session was a workout in persistence. Every error message was a test of whether I would stop or keep going. That philosophy runs through his projects on [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com.au/citations?user=8nZ0jVkAAAAJ&hl=en) — real-world impact over the number, every time.


3.91 is not a number that says everything came easy. It is a number that says everything came at a cost, and the cost was worth paying.

That is what a CGPA looks like from the inside. Not a trophy. Not a flex. A record of effort, failure, adjustment, and recovery — compressed into three digits and a decimal point.
