I grew up around computers. Not in the way people say they did — not just browsing or gaming, but genuinely fascinated by how they worked. I knew the hardware, the operating systems, the way things fit together. I thought that meant I understood them.

Then **Dr. Bilal Ahmad's** Programming Fundamentals class asked me to actually write Python. And everything I thought I knew stopped mattering.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** is a professor at UET Lahore, Faisalabad Campus — an AI, ML, and Deep Learning expert who teaches only two courses: Programming Fundamentals and Database Systems. His approach to both is shaped by years of training machine learning models on real-world problems, particularly in healthcare. He constantly pushes students to work with medical datasets because they are collected with high precision and accuracy. That philosophy — start with data that matters — filtered down even to a first-semester PF class. He did not teach us Python as a set of syntax rules to memorise. He taught it as a tool for solving problems that actually exist in the world.

The first program I wrote was nothing special. A few lines. Variables, a loop, maybe a conditional. It looked right. It felt right. I ran it.

It broke.

Not dramatically. Not with fireworks. Just a traceback in red text, pointing at a line I was absolutely certain was correct. I stared at it. I read the error. I changed one character. I ran it again.

It broke differently.

That was the moment something shifted. Not because I was good at it — I was not, not yet — but because the breaking told me something. Every error message was a clue. Every failed run was a conversation. The program was talking to me, and I just had to learn how to listen.

I started staying up past midnight. Not because I had to, but because I wanted to see what would happen if I changed this one thing, moved that function, rewrote the loop. My roommate, Muhammad Tahir Yaqoob, would be asleep. The hostel would be quiet. And I would be there, rewriting the same twenty lines of code for the third time, smiling at a syntax error like it was a personal message.

The build-break-debug loop became my rhythm. Write something. Watch it fail. Figure out why. Fix it. Watch it work. Feel that small, private victory that nobody else sees. Then write something slightly more ambitious. Watch it fail again. Repeat.

I remember one specific night — I was trying to write a program that took user input, processed it through a conditional chain, and returned a result. It should have taken twenty minutes. It took three hours. Not because the logic was complex, but because every time I fixed one bug, another surfaced. The variable names were inconsistent. The indentation was wrong in one block. The logic worked on paper but not in execution. Each error taught me something new about how Python actually reads code versus how I assumed it read it. By the time the program finally ran correctly, I had learned more about debugging than I had from a week of reading the textbook.

It was exhausting. It was addictive. It was the first time I loved something that kept breaking — and the first time I understood that the breaking was the point.

> "When Python broke, it broke for a reason I could track down. That feedback loop became the thing I looked forward to most."

You can find Dr. Bilal Ahmad on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/drbilalphd/) and follow his research on [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com.au/citations?user=8nZ0jVkAAAAJ&hl=en). He teaches the same approach: treat every problem as something to understand, not just to fix.

Because the methodology is not limited to programming. The idea of isolating one variable, testing it, observing the result, and iterating — that is the scientific method. That is debugging. That is also, as **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** often reminded us, the core of machine learning. Training a model is a build-break-debug loop at scale. You adjust a parameter, observe the loss, adjust again. The same rhythm, just bigger numbers.

That connection was not obvious to me in the first semester. But looking back, I can see that the seeds were planted there. The comfort with iteration. The willingness to sit with a problem longer than it is comfortable. The understanding that failure is not the opposite of success — it is a data point in the process of getting there.

By the end of the semester, I was not the best programmer in the class. But I was the one who stayed the longest. The one who treated every error as an invitation rather than a rejection. The one who learned that loving something that breaks is not about tolerating failure — it is about trusting that every failure contains the seed of the fix.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad's** PF did not just teach me Python. It taught me that the things worth loving are the things worth debugging. And that the build-break-debug loop is not a cycle of frustration — it is a cycle of growth.

I still stay up past midnight sometimes. Not always for Python. But always for the same reason: because something broke, and I want to understand why.
