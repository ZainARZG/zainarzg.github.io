I grew up around computers. Not in the way people say they did — not just browsing or gaming, but genuinely fascinated by how they worked. I knew the hardware, the operating systems, the way things fit together. I thought that meant I understood them.

Then Dr. Bilal's Programming Fundamentals class asked me to actually write Python.

And everything I thought I knew stopped mattering.

The first program I wrote was nothing special. A few lines. Variables, a loop, maybe a conditional. It looked right. It felt right. I ran it.

It broke.

Not dramatically. Not with fireworks. Just a traceback in red text, pointing at a line I was absolutely certain was correct. I stared at it. I read the error. I changed one character. I ran it again.

It broke differently.

That was the moment something shifted. Not because I was good at it — I was not, not yet — but because the breaking told me something. Every error message was a clue. Every failed run was a conversation. The program was talking to me, and I just had to learn how to listen.

I started staying up past midnight. Not because I had to, but because I wanted to see what would happen if I changed this one thing, moved that function, rewrote the loop. My roommate, Muhammad Tahir Yaqoob, would be asleep. The hostel would be quiet. And I would be there, rewriting the same twenty lines of code for the third time, smiling at a syntax error like it was a personal message.

The build-break-debug loop became my rhythm. Write something. Watch it fail. Figure out why. Fix it. Watch it work. Feel that small, private victory that nobody else sees. Then write something slightly more ambitious. Watch it fail again. Repeat.

It was exhausting. It was addictive. It was the first time I loved something that kept breaking — and the first time I understood that the breaking was the point.

> "When Python broke, it broke for a reason I could track down. That feedback loop became the thing I looked forward to most."

By the end of the semester, I was not the best programmer in the class. But I was the one who stayed the longest. The one who treated every error as an invitation rather than a rejection. The one who learned that loving something that breaks is not about tolerating failure — it is about trusting that every failure contains the seed of the fix.

Dr. Bilal's PF did not just teach me Python. It taught me that the things worth loving are the things worth debugging. And that the build-break-debug loop is not a cycle of frustration — it is a cycle of growth.

I still stay up past midnight sometimes. Not always for Python. But always for the same reason: because something broke, and I want to understand why.
