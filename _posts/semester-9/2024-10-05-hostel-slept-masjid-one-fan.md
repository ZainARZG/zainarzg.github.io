We had beds. That was supposed to be the victory.

After the chaos of Sunday — after pushing through crowds, after being told both hostels were full, after being called "the last ones" — we finally had a room. A real room with two beds and a roof and a door that locked from the inside. We had made it. We were inside.

Then we opened the door.

The room was dirty. Not the kind of dirty you can ignore, not a layer of dust you can sweep under the bed and forget about. It was the kind of dirty that tells you nobody has lived here in months, that the last occupants left in a hurry, that the windows have been sealed shut through an entire summer. The floor was stained. The walls had marks. The air inside was heavy, stale, as if the room had been breathing the same breath since June.

And the fan. There was one fan. Ceiling-mounted, old, with a wobble that made it look like it might detach at any moment. We switched it on. It turned. Slowly. The kind of slow where you can count the blades as they pass. It moved air, technically, but not enough to change anything. The room remained hot, sticky, unwelcoming.

We did not complain. After seeing the hall down the corridor — the one with ten to fifteen students on mattresses on the floor — complaining felt wrong. We had a room. We had beds. The dirt and the fan were our problem to solve.

Except we could not solve them right away. Because the room was locked.

The seniors had locked it.

I do not know exactly why. Maybe it was a tradition. Maybe they wanted to make sure the new students checked in through the proper process. Maybe they just wanted to establish that they were in charge. Whatever the reason, we stood outside that room for six to seven hours, bags at our feet, waiting for someone to come and unlock it.

Six to seven hours is a long time when you have nowhere to sit. The corridor was crowded, filled with other students in the same situation, some of them sitting on their luggage, others pacing, others trying to call someone who might know someone who had a key. We leaned against the wall, watched the chaos, and waited. No one came. The door stayed locked.

That night, we slept in the masjid.

The university masjid was cool, quiet, a world away from the noise of the hostel. The tiles were clean. There was space. Students who could not get into their rooms — students whose rooms were locked, students whose rooms were too dirty to sleep in, students who simply had nowhere else to go — had gathered there, spreading out sheets and blankets on the floor. It was not comfortable. The floor was hard. The lights stayed on all night. But it was cool, and it was quiet, and it was enough.

I lay there on the floor of the masjid, looking up at the ceiling, and thought about the day. The bee bite. The crowd. The race for beds. The dirty room. The locked door. The six-hour wait. It had been two days. We had been here for two days, and already the experience had been more intense than anything I had expected.

The next day, we went to university.

Classes had not fully started yet — we were still in the orientation window, the limbo between arrival and academics — but we walked to the campus anyway, found our departments, met some of the faculty. The buildings were clean, air-conditioned, organised. It felt like a different world from the hostel. The classroom was where everything made sense. The hostel was where everything fell apart.

When we returned that evening, the room was open.

The seniors had finally unlocked it, or someone from the administration had come, or the lock had been removed — I never learned the details. What mattered was that the door was open, and we could go in.

We cleaned the room that night. Not thoroughly — we did not have cleaning supplies, and the shops near the hostel were already closed — but enough. We swept the floor with a broken broom we found in the corridor. We wiped the surfaces with a damp cloth. We opened the window and let whatever air was moving outside come in. The fan still wobbled. The room was still hot. But it was ours.

That room became our home for the entire first semester.

We did not move. We did not request a change. We stayed in that same room — the one with the dirty floor and the one slow fan and the walls that had seen a hundred students before us — for every day of the first semester. We adjusted. We made it work. The dirt became familiar. The fan became background noise. The room stopped being a problem and started being where we lived.

And then winter came.

Faisalabad winters are not extreme, but they are cold enough to make a room without heating feel like a refrigerator. The fan that had been our enemy for months suddenly became irrelevant. We needed warmth. So we bought a heater. A small, electric, no-name heater that glowed orange when you switched it on and clicked when it reached temperature. It sat in the corner of the room, casting a faint orange light across the floor, pushing back the cold inch by inch.

We also bought a chulha.

I do not know the English word everyone uses for it. An electric stove? A hot plate? A single-burner cooking surface that you plug into the wall and that glows red when it heats up. Whatever you call it, we bought one, and it changed everything. Suddenly we could make tea. We could boil eggs. We could heat leftovers. The chulha sat on a small table we found somewhere, next to a bag of rice and a box of tea bags and a jar of sugar. The room that had been just a place to sleep became something more. It became a place to live.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** says in his ML lectures that constraints are not bugs — they are features. A model trained on limited data learns to generalise better because it has to. A model trained with limited compute becomes efficient because it cannot brute-force its way through the problem. The same principle applied to that room. We had one fan. We had no heating until we bought a heater. We had no kitchen until we bought a chulha. The constraints forced us to adapt, and adapting made us better at living on our own than we would have been if everything had been handed to us.

That heater and that chulha — those two small appliances — became the symbols of our first semester. The heater for survival. The chulha for independence. Together, they turned a dirty room with one broken fan into something that felt like ours.

By the time the first semester ended, I did not want to leave that room.

It was still small. It was still imperfect. The fan still wobbled. But it was ours. We had cleaned it. We had heated it. We had cooked in it. We had slept in it for months. It was not the room we had wanted. It was the room we had made, and that was worth more.
