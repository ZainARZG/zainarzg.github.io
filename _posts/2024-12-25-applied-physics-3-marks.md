Three marks.

Out of however many the quiz was worth. It does not matter what the total was. Three marks is three marks, and three marks is a number that sits on your result sheet like an accusation.

> "Three marks. Not from not trying. From trying the wrong way."

Here is the thing about Applied Physics: it is not Programming Fundamentals. It is not Database Systems. It is a theory-heavy, concept-dense subject that does not care how good you are at writing code. It cares whether you understand the physics, and understanding the physics requires a completely different approach from what I was used to.

I walked into that quiz prepared the way I prepare for everything else. I reviewed the material. I read through my notes. I thought I knew enough.

I did not.

The quiz asked questions that required deep conceptual understanding, not surface-level familiarity. It asked me to connect ideas I had learned separately. It asked me to apply principles to situations I had not seen before. And I sat there, knowing that I had studied, knowing that I had tried, and knowing that trying the wrong way is functionally identical to not trying at all.

Three marks.

I could have let that define me. I could have decided that physics was not my subject, that I was a programming person, that theory was not my thing. A lot of people would have.

Instead, I did the only thing that made sense: I changed how I studied.

I stopped reading passively and started solving actively. I stopped assuming that familiarity meant understanding and started testing myself on every concept. I went to the textbook, not just the slides. I worked through problems I had not been assigned. I asked questions in class that probably revealed how much I did not know, and I asked them anyway.

It was not glamorous. It was not a dramatic turnaround story where I suddenly became a physics genius overnight. It was slow, methodical, unexciting work. The kind of work that does not make for a good story but does make for better results.

By the time finals arrived, I was not the same person who had scored three marks on that quiz. I was someone who had learned how to study a subject that demanded something different from me. Someone who had adjusted. Someone who had grown.

I got an A- in the finals.

An A- does not erase the three marks. It does not pretend they never happened. It sits right next to them, and together they tell a more honest story than either one could alone.

The three marks say: you approached this wrong. The A- says: you figured out how to approach it right.

And the space between them is where the actual learning happened.

Applied Physics taught me more than physics. It taught me that the method matters as much as the effort. That studying hard is useless if you are studying the wrong way. That adjustment is not a sign of weakness — it is the entire point of education.

I still think about that quiz sometimes. Not with embarrassment. With gratitude. Because three marks was the wake-up call I needed, and what came after was the growth I had been looking for.
