The mids had not gone the way I wanted. The DBS midterm was long, the quiz was stacked into the same timeframe, and the score — 20 out of 30 — reflected the disruption more than the understanding I knew I had. It was honest feedback, but it left a gap between what I could do and what the paper had measured.

Then **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** did something that surprised the entire batch. He announced two quizzes.

Not one. Two.

The first was on relational algebra — the formal foundation of database queries that lives beneath every SQL statement we write. The second was on SQL queries themselves — the practical application of everything we had studied in the first half of the semester. They were separate assessments, each with its own focus, and together they covered both the theory and the practice of querying a database.

What made this different from the midterm was the time.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** gave us significant time to complete both quizzes. Not the compressed, stacked format of the midterm where every second felt like it was borrowed from the next question. This time, the clock was generous. There was enough time to read each question carefully, think through the answer, write it completely, and review it before moving on. The pressure was not about speed. It was about correctness.

I remember reading the relational algebra paper and feeling something I had not felt during the midterm: clarity. The questions were structured, the notation was familiar, and the problems were designed to test understanding rather than trick you into mistakes. I moved through the paper steadily, writing relational algebra expressions for queries I had only ever written in SQL. Converting between the two felt natural — the concepts were the same, just expressed differently.

By the time I reached the SQL quiz, I was in a rhythm. The queries ranged from basic SELECT statements to multi-table JOINs with grouping and aggregation. I had practiced enough of these during the project — building the e-commerce database schema, writing queries for the product catalogue, designing the order management system — that the patterns were already embedded. I wrote the answers quickly, checked them once, and realised I had finished before the allotted time.

The silence in the room was uneven. On my side of the hall, the scratching of pens had already slowed down — a few of us had reached the end. On the other side, the sound was continuous, urgent, punctuated by the occasional eraser scraping across paper. I did not want to make it obvious that I was done, so I sat still, pretending to review my answers while actually watching the clock and wondering why the gap existed.

I looked around the room. Most of my classmates were still writing.

Some of them looked stressed. A few were flipping between pages, erasing answers, rewriting them. The clock was not a problem for me, but it clearly was for others. I sat there, done, waiting for the submission time — and I genuinely could not understand why some students wanted even more time.

Not because I thought the quiz was easy. It was not. The questions covered real depth — relational algebra operators like selection, projection, join, division; SQL queries that required careful thinking about GROUP BY and HAVING clauses; edge cases in JOIN behaviour that you only catch if you truly understand how the database engine processes them. But the time was fair. More than fair. It was generous.

There was one question in particular — a relational algebra query that asked us to find suppliers who supplied all parts of a certain type. It looked like a division operation, but the way the problem was framed, most students tried to solve it with a join and a subquery instead. I had seen that exact pattern during the e-commerce project when I needed to find products that had been ordered by every customer in a specific city. The relational algebra expression was clean — a division followed by a projection — but only because I had written the SQL equivalent weeks earlier and understood why a division was the correct operation.

That contrast — between my experience and my classmates' — taught me something about preparation. The reason I finished early was not that I was smarter. It was that the e-commerce project had forced me to write real queries against a real database, with real data, under real constraints. Every time I designed a table, I was practicing relational schema design. Every time I wrote a query for the project, I was reinforcing the same patterns the quiz was testing. The preparation had happened in the weeks before, not the night before.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** teaches the same lesson in his ML lectures: a model generalises well only when it has been trained on diverse, representative data. The same is true for students. The reason some of my classmates struggled with the time was not that they were unprepared — it was that their preparation had been narrower. They had studied the theory but had not applied it enough. The project had given me the applied hours that the quiz was rewarding.

The quizzes were not the end of the semester. They were a midpoint checkpoint — a way for Dr. Bilal to see who had truly internalised the material and who needed more practice before the final exam. And in that sense, they worked exactly as designed. For the students who finished early, the message was clear: keep going, the foundation is solid. For the ones who needed more time, the message was equally clear: the project is not just an assignment — it is your training ground.

Follow him on [Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/Dr.BilalAhm). His approach to assessment has always been diagnostic — the quizzes were not about catching anyone out. They were about revealing what each student actually understood, so the remainder of the semester could fill the gaps.

I walked out of that quiz session feeling something I had missed since the mids started: confidence. Not arrogance — just the quiet certainty that I had learned what the course had asked me to learn, and the quizzes had simply confirmed it.
