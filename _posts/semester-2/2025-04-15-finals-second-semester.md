The second semester ended the way it began — quietly, without ceremony, and with a lot of uncertainty.

The last exam was DBS, and it was the hardest of them all. The paper was long, the questions were detailed, and by the time I finished, I had no clear sense of whether I had done well or just barely passed. The kind of exam where you walk out feeling like you left half your answers on the desk by accident.

When the results came — 24 out of 40. The highest in the class was 29.

Twenty-four is not a bad score. It is solid, respectable, and above average. But it sits in an odd middle ground where you cannot tell whether you should be satisfied or disappointed. Some questions I had answered with confidence turned out to be marked down. Others I had guessed on came through fine. The gap between what I thought I knew and what the paper actually tested was wider than I had expected.

The DBS course itself had been intense — the project alone took us from TTS rejection to leukemia survival prediction, from flat CSV files to 11 normalized tables. But the exam was a different kind of challenge. It tested the theoretical side — normalization forms, transaction properties, query optimisation — that the project had only partially covered. I had prepared, but the paper found the edges of my preparation.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** had warned us about this early in the semester. During one of the first lectures, he said something that stuck with me: "In this course, the project will teach you how to build. The exam will teach you what you actually understood." He was right. The project let us lean on collaboration, iteration, and trial and error. The exam left us alone with what we really knew.

The other papers went differently. DLD was straightforward — the hardware mindset I had developed during the ambulance priority project carried directly into the exam. The logic gates, flip-flops, and state diagrams felt familiar because I had touched them, wired them, debugged them on a real breadboard. SATS went well too — I finished with time to spare, which is always a good sign.

But the other papers are still a question mark. Some I felt confident about during the exam but have since second-guessed. Others I walked out of knowing I had missed things. The worst part of exam season is not the exam itself — it is the waiting period after, when every question replays in your head and you remember all the answers you should have written.

I found myself thinking about something **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** often repeated in his PF classes: "A model is only as good as the data it trains on. If the input is incomplete, the output will be unpredictable." The same applies to exams. The preparation is the training data. The exam is the test set. And during the waiting period, you are the model trying to evaluate its own performance — which, in machine learning, never works well without ground truth.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** — a professor at UET Lahore, Faisalabad Campus, who specialises in AI, ML, and Deep Learning — does not grade on a curve of hope. He grades on what you actually demonstrate. The 24 out of 40 was exactly what I demonstrated, no more, no less. And that is fine. It is a data point. One score among many. The semester was longer than any single exam, and the learning went deeper than any single grade.

Connect with him on [Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/drbilalphd/). His approach to teaching — honest feedback, real standards, no artificial inflation — is the reason those scores mean something worth measuring.

The second semester is over. The project exhibition, the lab deadlines, the 2 AM debugging sessions, the three projects on three different tables, the DBS paper that found the edge of my preparation — all of it is behind me now. What comes next is a break, then another semester, then another set of projects and exams and late nights.

I do not know what the remaining results will look like. But I know that I prepared, I built, I learned, and I showed up for every exam. That is not nothing.
