Second semester started in the hostel. It continued at home. And the transition happened so fast that I barely had time to process it.

It was a combination of things. Ramadan was coming up, which meant changed schedules and different rhythms. Online classes meant that physical presence on campus was no longer mandatory. Regional conflicts were making travel unpredictable. And petrol prices were making the bus option increasingly impractical.

All of these factors converged into a single decision: it was time to go home.

> "The train was cheaper. The razai fit easily. My home is near the station. Some things, occasionally, just go right."

The train was the obvious choice. Cheaper than the bus. More reliable than trying to coordinate a ride. And it had one advantage that no other option could match: I could bring the razai.

For anyone who has not experienced a Pakistani winter in a hostel, the razai is not just a blanket. It is the difference between sleeping and not sleeping. It is the difference between comfort and spending the entire night shivering under three thin sheets that do not touch each other properly. My razai was essential. And the train was the only way to bring it home without paying extra for luggage.

The journey itself was uneventful, which is the best thing you can say about a journey. The train ran on time. The seat was available. The razai fit without argument. I got off at the station near my home in Bhalwal and walked the short distance from the platform to the front door.

Some things, occasionally, just go right.

Being home after months in the hostel is a particular kind of experience. The hostel becomes your life — your routine, your social circle, your identity. You forget what it feels like to sleep in your own bed, to eat food that was not chosen from a limited hostel menu, to sit in a room that does not have a roommate who is also trying to study. My friends Abdullah Khalid and Abdullah Sikandar were part of that hostel life. They were the people I studied with, complained with, and occasionally just sat in silence with during those late-night sessions when nobody had the energy to talk but nobody wanted to be alone either.

Leaving them was not easy. But going home was not a loss. It was a recalibration.

At home, I could study in my own space. I could attend online classes without the hostel's unreliable internet. I could focus without the ambient chaos of communal living. And I could do it all with the razai, which is not a small thing.

One of the classes I continued online was **Dr. Bilal Ahmad's** Database Systems course. The transition from in-person to online was smoother than I expected — the lectures were structured, the slides were clear, and **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** made sure we stayed on track despite the disruption. I would sit at my desk in Bhalwal, notebook open, building ER diagrams and writing SQL queries while the train sounds from the station drifted through the window. The setting had changed completely, but the learning had not skipped a beat.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** — a professor at UET Lahore, Faisalabad Campus, who specialises in AI, ML, and Deep Learning — had always emphasised that real engineering work happens wherever you are. In ML, your environment changes constantly — different datasets, different hardware, different deployment contexts. If your model cannot adapt to a new environment, it is not a robust model. The same applied to us as students. If we could not adapt to a new learning environment, we were not ready for the field.

That idea stayed with me throughout the transition. The hostel had been my learning environment for months. Now home was. The change was physical, but the work was the same — the DBS assignments, the SQL practice, the project planning. None of it stopped because I changed locations. It continued, adapted to the new setting.

I remember one particular afternoon — I was sitting at my desk, working through a set of SQL join queries that **Dr. Bilal** had assigned. The table relationships were complex, and I kept getting the wrong output. Outside my window, Bhalwal was quiet in that afternoon way that only small towns can be. Inside, I was debugging a query, trying to figure out why my left join was returning nulls where it should not have. The problem was the same one I would have faced in the hostel computer lab. The solution was the same process of elimination, the same systematic checking of assumptions. The location did not change the logic. It is the kind of adaptable thinking **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** emphasises on his [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/drbilalphd/) — learning that travels with you, wherever you are.

The second semester moved from the hostel to home in the space of a single week. One day I was in the hostel, and the next I was on a train, and the day after that I was at my desk at home, attending a DBS lecture online, with the razai on the chair beside me and the familiar walls of my room around me.

It was not the plan I had started the semester with. But it was the plan that worked. And sometimes the best plans are the ones you do not make — the ones that circumstances make for you, and you just have the sense to accept.

Bhalwal feels different after being away. Quieter. Slower. More intentional. But the work that matters — the DBS projects, the ML models, the learning — happens wherever I am. The train ride home was not an interruption. It was a transition between two versions of the same learning journey, and both of them matter.
