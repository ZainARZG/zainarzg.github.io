The first idea was not the right one

When Database Systems began in second semester, I did not first connect with it through SQL syntax. My first memorable connection with the course came through a rejected project idea. We had initially been asked to build a website-based project using tools like WordPress, XAMPP or WAMP, and MySQL. I suggested a dropshipping-style e-commerce idea based on sourcing products through larger platforms. At the time it felt practical to me, but my teacher rejected it because the course direction was focused on products or businesses that actually exist in our cities or local context.

At first, that rejection felt frustrating because I had already started imagining how the idea might work. But I also knew the objection made sense within the assignment. It was one of those moments where the problem is not that the idea is impossible. The problem is that it does not fit the frame you have been given. That matters in technical work. A solution can be clever and still be wrong for the actual requirement.

What the rejection changed in my thinking

After that conversation, the direction shifted. My teacher suggested either blog-based work with provided keywords or moving toward AI, ML, or deep learning. I chose the AI direction because it aligned more naturally with my interest. Even before the course settled fully, that experience taught me something useful about project work: sometimes progress comes from releasing your first idea instead of protecting it. That is not always easy, especially when you have already attached energy to the original plan.

It was also my first time seriously imagining that I might need to handle a project more independently. Later, because of changing rules around group size, I ended up working with Muhammad Umair, but the brief period where I thought I would do it alone mattered. It made me think more clearly about ownership, direction, and how I respond when things become uncertain. That is a different kind of learning than simply following a comfortable group structure from the beginning.

How SQL started making more sense afterward

What I like about Database Systems now is that SQL feels connected to a clearer form of thinking. Once I moved past the rejected idea, the subject started making more sense to me as a discipline of structure. Tables, relationships, and queries are not just technical details. They train a way of organizing information that is clean, logical, and precise. That is one reason DBS continues to interest me. It has the same honesty that attracted me to PF, but it expresses it through data instead of code flow.

So when I say Database Systems began with rejection for me, I do not mean the course started badly. I mean it started with a correction. That correction helped me understand the course more seriously. It reminded me that good engineering work is not only about having ideas. It is also about matching ideas to context, refining them when necessary, and learning to value structure instead of only novelty. SQL arrived after that lesson, and I think I understood it better because of the rejection that came first.
