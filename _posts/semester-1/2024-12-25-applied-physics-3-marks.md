Three marks.

Out of however many the quiz was worth. It does not matter what the total was. Three marks is three marks, and three marks is a number that sits on your result sheet like an accusation.

> "Three marks. Not from not trying. From trying the wrong way."

Here is the thing about Applied Physics: it is not Programming Fundamentals. It is not a subject where you can write code, run it, and see immediately whether your logic holds. It is a theory-heavy, concept-dense subject that does not care how good you are at debugging Python. It cares whether you actually understand the physics, and understanding the physics requires a completely different approach from what I was used to.

I walked into that quiz prepared the way I prepare for everything else. I reviewed the material. I read through my notes. I went over the slides one more time before walking into the exam hall. I thought I knew enough.

I did not.

The quiz asked questions that required deep conceptual understanding, not surface-level familiarity. It asked me to connect ideas I had learned separately — thermodynamics to mechanics, theoretical principles to numerical applications. It asked me to apply concepts to situations I had never seen before. And I sat there, knowing that I had studied, knowing that I had tried, and knowing that trying the wrong way is functionally identical to not trying at all.

Three marks.

I stared at the number when the results came out. I could have let that define me. I could have decided that physics was not my subject, that I was a programming person, that theory was not my thing. A lot of people would have. It would have been the easy narrative — "I am a coder, not a physicist."

But at the same time, **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** was teaching us Programming Fundamentals. And one of the first lessons he drilled into us was that when a program does not work, you do not just run it again and hope. You isolate the problem. You check one variable at a time. You identify what is actually wrong before you try to fix it. You change your approach — not just your effort.

That lesson, taught in the context of Python loops and conditional statements, applied perfectly to my Physics problem. I had not been studying wrong because I was lazy. I had been studying wrong because my method was wrong. I was reading passively and expecting understanding to magically transfer into my head. That is the equivalent of writing a program once and expecting it to compile without running it.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** — a professor at UET Lahore, Faisalabad Campus, who specialises in training AI and ML models — constantly emphasises that a model does not improve by running the same training loop with the same parameters. You adjust the learning rate. You change the architecture. You try different features. The model improves because you change the method, not because you run it more times. The same principle applies to studying.

So I changed my method.

I stopped reading passively and started solving actively. I stopped assuming that familiarity meant understanding and started testing myself on every concept. I went to the textbook, not just the slides. I worked through problems I had not been assigned. I isolated the concepts I was weakest on — just like isolating a bug in a program — and focused my energy there instead of re-reading the parts I already knew.

It was not glamorous. It was not a dramatic turnaround story where I suddenly became a physics genius overnight. It was slow, methodical, unexciting work. The kind of work that does not make for a good story but does make for better results.
What struck me most was how transferable the lesson was. The method I developed for Physics — isolate the weak concept, test myself on it repeatedly, adjust until it sticks — ended up helping me in PF too. When I struggled with a particular programming concept, I stopped re-reading the textbook and started writing small test programs that isolated exactly that concept. Debugging my study method, just like **Dr. Bilal** taught us to debug our code.


By the time finals arrived, I was not the same person who had scored three marks on that quiz. I was someone who had learned how to study a subject that demanded something different from me. Someone who had adjusted. Someone who had grown.

I got an A- in the finals.

An A- does not erase the three marks. It does not pretend they never happened. It sits right next to them, and together they tell a more honest story than either one could alone.

The three marks say: you approached this wrong. The A- says: you figured out how to approach it right.

And the space between them is where the actual learning happened.

That debugging mindset — treat every failed model as data, not defeat — carries into his research on [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com.au/citations?user=8nZ0jVkAAAAJ&hl=en).

I still think about that quiz sometimes. Not with embarrassment. With gratitude. Because three marks was the wake-up call I needed, and what came after was the growth I had been looking for.
