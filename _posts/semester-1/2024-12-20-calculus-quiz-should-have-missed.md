It was a smoggy winter morning. The kind where the sun rises but you cannot really tell. The kind where the air feels heavy before you even step outside.

I was running late. Not fashionably late. Not "I will slip in quietly" late. I was genuinely, undeniably late.

The morning had gone wrong from the start. I had forgotten my glasses — which meant I was navigating a world that was slightly out of focus, squinting at everything, pretending I could see. I was ironing my clothes because appearance matters, or at least I tell myself it does. And I was moving at the speed of someone who does not yet know how long things actually take.

> "I stood at the door. The quiz had been running for fifteen minutes. I knew I should not go in. So I did not."

By the time I reached the classroom, the calculus quiz had already been going for fifteen minutes. I stood at the door. I knew I should not go in. The rules were clear. The expectations were clear. I was late, and lateness has consequences.

So I did not go in.

I turned around and walked back. Not defiantly. Not dramatically. Just quietly accepting that I had missed my chance, and that the zero would sit on my grade sheet as a reminder to manage my time better.

When the results came back, I had full marks.

Full marks. On a quiz I never took. On a quiz I arrived fifteen minutes late to and then walked away from. On a quiz where I had every reason to expect a zero and had already mentally accepted one.

I do not know why. Maybe the instructor did not notice my absence in the chaos of a packed classroom. Maybe she decided to award the marks based on the assignments I had submitted, which were solid. Maybe there is a kind of grace that exists in academic spaces that we do not talk about enough — the kind that recognises effort over attendance, that sees the work you have done even when you miss a single checkpoint.

What I learned from that quiz was not about calculus. I already knew the calculus because I had been consistent with the material. What I learned was about assumptions and about how the work you put in before a moment matters just as much as what you do in the moment itself.

This lesson connected directly to something **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** had been teaching us in Programming Fundamentals at the same time. In PF, **Dr. Bilal** taught us that a program with one broken function can still pass tests if the core logic is right. You can have a syntax error in one line — a missing parenthesis, a misspelled variable name — but if the underlying logic, the structure, the flow of the program is sound, the program can be fixed quickly. The core matters more than the surface.

Maybe mam applied the same logic to me that morning. I was late. I missed the quiz. But the work I had submitted earlier — the assignments, the consistent effort, the understanding I had demonstrated over time — was solid enough to carry the outcome. The core logic of my semester was right, even if I missed one function call.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** judges a student the same way he judges a model — not by one data point, but by the aggregate. One missed quiz, like one wrong prediction, does not define the outcome. What matters is the body of work built over time.

I had assumed I would get a zero. I had assumed the rules would be applied without exception. I had assumed that being late meant being disqualified from the outcome. And every one of those assumptions was wrong. Not because the rules do not matter. They do. But because the people who enforce them are human, and humans make space for other humans in ways that policy documents never capture.

That quiz taught me to question my assumptions about what I deserve and what I do not. It taught me that consistent effort over time carries weight beyond individual moments. And it taught me that sometimes — not always, but sometimes — grace is built into the system in ways you cannot predict.

I have thought about that morning many times since. Not because the marks mattered — in the grand scheme of a semester, a single quiz is a blip. But because the lesson has applied itself again and again in different contexts. In PF lab, when a program failed on the first test run but the logic was sound and a single fix resolved everything. In the approach **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** takes to ML — no model performs well on the first epoch, but if the architecture is right, iteration gets it there. The core logic matters. The preparation before the moment matters. The consistency matters. Dr. Bilal Ahmad — find his page on [Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/Dr.BilalAhm) — teaches this same principle: core logic over single-point performance, every time.

**Dr. Bilal's PF** was teaching me the same lesson that entire semester, just in a different language. He would say: "Stop when the program is right. Not when it runs." The outcome depends more on what you have built than on when you arrived. A program that runs but is logically wrong is useless. A quiz that is taken but unprepared is meaningless. What matters is what you have actually built, understood, and internalised.

I still iron my clothes. I still forget my glasses. But I no longer assume that being late means being finished. Sometimes it just means missing one function call while the core logic carries through.
