Mid-semester, a groupmate transferred to another campus. No falling out, no incident — just an opportunity, taken. The transfer was clean and final.

What followed was quieter than I expected. Nobody called a meeting. Nobody reassigned roles. People just started filling what needed filling — someone tracked deadlines more carefully, another shared updates in the group. The work did not stop. It changed shape.

That kind of quiet adaptability reminded me of something **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** had been teaching us in Programming Fundamentals around the same time. He would assign lab tasks with incomplete code — a missing function, a broken import, an error that only appeared under specific conditions. The point was never to complain about what was missing. It was to work with what was there and build from it.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** — a professor at UET Lahore, Faisalabad Campus, who trains machine learning models on real-world problems — approaches datasets the same way. A medical dataset has missing values, inconsistent labels, corrupted entries. The model does not stop training because the data is imperfect. You clean it. You adjust the pipeline. You continue.

I remember one lab session where he gave us a partially written program — half the functions were just stubs with placeholder comments. Some of us started complaining about what was missing. He stopped us and said something I have not forgotten: "A dataset is never perfect. A real-world problem never comes with complete code. Start with what you have, and build from there."

That is exactly what the batch did when the groupmate left. Nobody handed us a complete coordination structure. We had what we had. We built from there.

Looking back, the most valuable thing I gained that semester was not a specific programming skill or a grade. It was the understanding that adaptability is a separate skill from technical competence — and possibly a more important one. You can be the best programmer in the class, but if you cannot adjust when the requirements change, you will struggle in every group project, every job, every real-world scenario.

Follow Dr. Bilal Ahmad on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/drbilalphd/). His teaching philosophy — real-world mentoring over textbook theory — reflects this adaptability mindset.

The groupmate who left probably does not know that their departure reinforced this lesson. It was not personal. It was structural. Someone left. We adjusted. The lesson has applied itself again and again since — when a program throws an unfamiliar error, when a model does not converge, when a project requirement shifts mid-way. The question is always the same: what needs to happen next?
