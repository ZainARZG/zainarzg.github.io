The laptop needed repair. Not the kind of repair you can do yourself — not a restart, not a reinstall, not any of the things you try before admitting that you need professional help. This was hardware. This was a trip to the city. This was a full-day commitment.

The stakes felt higher than they might have been for someone else. This laptop was not just a device. It was the machine I used for everything that mattered that semester — including **Dr. Bilal Ahmad's** Programming Fundamentals class. Every Python program I wrote, every lab task I submitted, every error message I debugged past midnight — it all lived on this machine. Without it, I could not attend online classes. I could not complete PF assignments. I could not do any of the work that my semester depended on.

I am from Bhalwal. Lahore is not my city. I know the routes people take when they travel regularly, but I do not know the shortcuts. I do not know which bus goes where. I do not know which shops are reliable and which ones will take your money and your time and give you nothing back.

What I do know is how to navigate a day when nothing goes according to plan.

The day started early. I took the first available transport to Lahore, assuming I would find a repair shop quickly, get the issue diagnosed, and be back before evening. That assumption lasted about an hour. The first shop said the part was not available. The second quoted a price that was more than I had. The third was willing to do the repair but could not guarantee it would be done the same day. Each rejection added time and distance to the itinerary.

At some point during those ten hours, I realized I had not eaten. Not because there was no food available — Lahore has food everywhere — but because the logistics of the day did not include a proper meal. I was moving from shop to shop, negotiating prices, waiting for diagnostics, carrying a broken laptop through a city I did not know well. What I had was a single packet of grains that I had brought from the hostel. That was it. That was the entire day's nutrition.

> "A packet of grains for the whole day. Then 4km on foot. Then a proper meal. Then I opened the laptop — just to check everything was still there."

The bus back to Faisalabad was sold out. Not delayed. Not late. Sold out. Every seat taken. And I was standing at the station with a repaired laptop and no way home.

So I walked.

Four kilometres. On foot. Through Faisalabad. With a laptop bag and a packet of grains and the particular kind of determination that comes from having already spent ten hours on something and refusing to let a sold-out bus be the thing that defeats you.

I made it home before midnight. Not triumphantly. Not heroically. Just... home. Tired, hungry, but home. And the first thing I did — before eating, before resting, before doing any of the sensible things a person should do after a day like that — was open the laptop.

Just to check everything was still there.

The screen lit up. The system booted. Everything worked. And in that moment, the ten hours, the packet of grains, the four kilometres, the sold-out bus — all of it felt worth it. Because the laptop was fixed, and the laptop is the tool that makes everything else possible. **Dr. Bilal's** PF assignments. The Python programs I was writing late at night. The lab tasks that needed to be submitted. The Honahaar Scholarship that depends on maintaining performance. All of it lives inside that machine.

I remember thinking about something **Dr. Bilal Ahmad** had said in class the week before. He was talking about how in machine learning, the quality of your output depends entirely on the quality of your input. Garbage in, garbage out. If your data is corrupted, your model will be corrupted. The same applies to hardware — if your machine is broken, your work stops. There is no way around it. You cannot write code on a laptop that does not boot. You cannot submit an assignment on a device that will not turn on.

**Dr. Bilal Ahmad** — a professor at UET Lahore, Faisalabad Campus, and an AI/ML expert — teaches his students to respect the tools of their work. A machine learning engineer maintains their dataset. A programmer maintains their development environment. And a first-semester student maintains their laptop, because without it, none of the learning happens. Connect with him on [Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/Dr.BilalAhm) — quality output starts with quality tools and data.

People talk about resilience like it is a grand quality. Like it is something you announce. But resilience is mostly small, unglamorous things: walking four kilometres because the bus is full. Eating a packet of grains because there is no time for anything else. Navigating a city you do not know because the alternative is giving up. And in this case, spending ten hours to repair a laptop because without it, the PF assignments would not get done.

I did not do any of it for the story. I did it because the laptop needed repair, and I was the one who had to get it repaired, and there was nobody else to do it.

That is what resilience actually looks like. Not a speech. Not a medal. Just a person, a laptop, a packet of grains, and four kilometres of road between the station and home.
